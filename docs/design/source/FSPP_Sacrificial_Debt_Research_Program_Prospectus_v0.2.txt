Executive Summary
This project asks a general political-pathological question generated by, but not reducible to, Richard A. Koenigsberg’s work on the sacrificial meaning of the Holocaust: under what conditions does perceived exemption from collective sacrifice become an unpayable political debt that legitimates coercive suffering or death of the allegedly nonsacrificing group? The project moves beyond validating Koenigsberg’s interpretation of Hitler. Its purpose is to identify, formalize, test, and delimit a broader mechanism by which unequal suffering can alter political deservingness, turn survival into evidence of guilt, and authorize escalating forms of exclusion and violence. The prospectus is designed to generate multiple linked scholarly outputs; a focused postgraduate paper is one empirical test within the broader program, not a miniature version of the entire project.
The project begins from a historically established phenomenon. First World War societies commonly developed moral languages of sacrifice in which hardship was expected to be shared, while “shirkers” and “profiteers” became objects of anger. In Germany, the 1916 Judenzählung institutionalized unfounded allegations that Jews were avoiding frontline service, despite substantial Jewish military participation. Koenigsberg’s work identifies a radicalized form of this logic in Hitler: German soldiers suffer and die; Jews are imagined as escaping the obligation to suffer and die; Jewish death is then represented as redressing an intolerable imbalance. The proposed research asks what political and psychological processes make that transition possible, and why comparable sacrificial resentment in other societies did not produce the same outcome.
The project therefore uses Germany as a mechanism-discovery case and compares it initially with Britain, Australia, and France, where mass mobilization, casualty burdens, accusations of shirking, and demands for “equality of sacrifice” were also present. The comparison is designed around variation in outcome: stigma and coercion toward alleged nonsacrificers occurred across these societies, but racialized, ontological, and exterminatory forms of sacrificial debt did not develop in the same way.
Central research question: Under what conditions does perceived exemption from collective sacrifice become an unpayable political debt that legitimates the coercive suffering or death of the allegedly nonsacrificing group?
Decision Summary
Treat this document as a comparative research-program prospectus with staged theory development, empirical pilots, comparative tests, and multiple scholarly outputs.
Designate the German-Jewish veteran study, “When Survival Becomes Guilt,” as the recommended postgraduate empirical paper because it provides a hard test of whether demonstrated sacrifice can discharge an accusation of nonsacrifice.
Treat “sacrificial debt” as a new theory-building project rather than a second Koenigsberg-validation workbench.
Use Hitler and the German-Jewish case to discover the mechanism, but require comparison with non-exterminatory cases before making general claims.
Begin with manual, proposition-level coding of a small high-quality corpus before building large-scale NLP or automated extraction.
Create falsification criteria at the start and maintain a dedicated negative-evidence record throughout the project.
Make the behavioral-to-ontological transition a central object of study: “you did not sacrifice” can potentially be corrected; “your kind does not sacrifice” may create an unpayable debt.
Treat genocide as one possible terminal outcome, not as the definition of sacrificial debt. The model must explain variation from stigma through exclusion, punitive extraction, coercive suffering, and extermination.
1. Project Rationale and Intellectual Problem
Koenigsberg’s interpretation of Hitler provides an unusually sharp observation: Hitler repeatedly moralized German military death and juxtaposed it with populations he imagined as escaping the sacrificial obligation. In Koenigsberg’s formulation, the Holocaust can be interpreted partly as an attempt to “balance the scales of death.” That claim is historically provocative, but simply assembling additional Hitler quotations that confirm the same proposition would produce a validation exercise rather than a new research program.
The larger problem appears when Koenigsberg’s observation is placed beside First World War scholarship. Historians of the war describe a “social relations of sacrifice” in which communities accepted hardship partly on the assumption that burdens were fairly shared. Those seen as avoiding service or profiting from others’ suffering could be cast outside the moral community. Australian conscription debates, for example, explicitly invoked the question “why should my son, father or husband die while yours lives?” British men who did not enlist could be publicly marked as shirkers or cowards. French wartime discourse contrasted combatants paying the “blood tax” with embusqués who appeared to escape the front. Germany’s Judenzählung translated a similar suspicion into an official inquiry aimed at Jewish military participation.
These findings suggest that unequal sacrifice is neither uniquely German nor uniquely Nazi. The new research problem is therefore not whether mass sacrifice creates resentment. It plainly can. The unexplained transition is how a remediable accusation of unequal contribution becomes a fixed moral identity, how survival itself can become incriminating, and how a perceived debt can become collectable through state coercion.
1.1 Why this is a Foundation for the Study of Political Pathology project
The project fits the Foundation’s broader concern with political orders that transform persons and bodies into resources for sustaining a collective sacred object. It also extends the Foundation’s distinction between offering and sacrifice. A soldier may offer, willingly or under compulsion, his body to the political community; political authority receives and disposes of that body through war. The proposed project examines the further pathology that arises when authorities or publics conclude that other persons have not offered enough, have survived unjustly, or owe suffering because others have already paid the blood price.
This shifts analysis from the familiar question “why does a regime hate an enemy?” to a different question: “what moral accounting system makes another person’s survival appear unjust?” That is a distinct problem in political morality and one that potentially links nationalism, citizenship, war, social deservingness, scapegoating, exclusion, and mass violence without collapsing those phenomena into one category.
2. Research Questions
2.1 Central research question
Under what conditions does perceived exemption from collective sacrifice become an unpayable political debt that legitimates the coercive suffering or death of the allegedly nonsacrificing group?
2.2 Subordinate questions
How does participation in collective sacrifice become a measure of political worth, loyalty, masculinity, citizenship, or national belonging?
How are sacrificers and survivors compared, and when does unequal survival become morally problematic?
When does the claim “you have not sacrificed” become the ontological claim “your kind does not or cannot sacrifice”?
How does a sacrificial deficit become a debt owed to the dead, to veterans, to bereaved families, to the nation, or to the state?
Who identifies the debtor population, narrates the debt, and claims authority to collect it?
Why does sacrificial resentment produce stigma or social coercion in some cases, political exclusion or punitive extraction in others, and exterminatory violence only in some?
Does failed sacrifice—especially catastrophic sacrifice followed by defeat—produce stronger demands to identify traitors, shirkers, profiteers, or groups alleged to have survived at others’ expense?
What evidence would distinguish sacrificial debt from ordinary prejudice, racial ideology, scapegoating, revenge, wartime brutalization, or material competition?
3. Novelty and Relationship to Existing Scholarship
The project’s novelty must be stated cautiously. Existing scholarship already covers many neighboring concepts: wartime “communities of sacrifice,” equal distribution of hardship, the moral economy of military deservingness, the cult of the fallen soldier, brutalization, redemptive antisemitism, the persistence of interethnic violence after 1918, and the relationship between total war and genocide. The project should therefore not claim that historians have ignored sacrifice, inequality, or resentment.
The provisional contribution is instead integrative and mechanistic: to explain how perceived unequal sacrifice can be converted into an attributed debt; how that debt can be essentialized at the group level; how evidence of actual sacrifice may fail to discharge it; and how political actors can transform the debt into a justification for coercion. Phase 1 must include a formal novelty audit designed to discover whether an equivalent theory already exists under other terminology. If it does, the project should revise its contribution rather than defend an artificial claim to originality.

4. Conceptual Framework
The proposal uses a small set of concepts that must remain analytically distinct. Their purpose is to prevent “sacrifice” from becoming a catch-all label for any suffering or death.
TABLE | Concept | Working definition
TABLE | Collective sacred object | The entity represented as possessing a claim superior to ordinary individual preservation: nation, people, revolution, race, state, faith, or other collective.
TABLE | Sacrificial demand | A claim that persons must surrender valued goods—time, property, bodily safety, family relations, freedom, or life—for the collective.
TABLE | Offering | The act by which a person or group yields something of value. It may be voluntary, socially compelled, legally compelled, or coerced.
TABLE | Sacrifice | The socially or politically interpreted surrender or destruction of something valuable for the sake of a higher object or claimed communal good.
TABLE | Sacrificial asymmetry | A perceived inequality in the distribution of risk, deprivation, suffering, or death undertaken for the collective.
TABLE | Sacrificial creditor | An actor or symbolic constituency—soldiers, veterans, bereaved families, “the fallen,” or the nation itself—whose sacrifice is represented as creating a claim.
TABLE | Sacrificial debtor | A person or group represented as owing contribution, suffering, or repayment because others have sacrificed more.
TABLE | Sacrificial debt | The attributed moral obligation arising from perceived unequal sacrifice.
TABLE | Sacrificial essentialization | The shift from an allegation about conduct (“you did not sacrifice”) to an allegation about group nature (“your kind does not or cannot sacrifice”).
TABLE | Unpayable debt | A debt whose alleged basis is ontological or racialized such that ordinary evidence of service, loyalty, or sacrifice cannot discharge it.
TABLE | Coercive sacrificial equalization | The imposition of deprivation, suffering, bodily risk, or death on an alleged debtor population in the name of correcting an unequal distribution of sacrifice.
TABLE | Exterminatory closure | The terminal claim that the debtor group’s continued existence itself prevents restoration, making removal or destruction appear necessary.
5. Proposed Causal Mechanism
The working mechanism is a sequence to be tested, not a presumed universal law. For comparative analysis, the twelve steps are grouped into four modules so that cases can be compared by where the pathway emerges, stalls, or changes form:
5.1 Mechanism modules
Module A – Sacrificial mobilization (steps 1–3): collective threat, visible surrender, and the conversion of sacrifice into a measure of virtue or membership.
Module B – Asymmetry and moral comparison (steps 4–7): identification of a lesser-sacrificing population, moralization of the difference, and attribution of a claim or debt.
Module C – Essentialization and political activation (steps 8–10): public construction of a debtor category, declining dischargeability of counterevidence, and institutional claims to collect the debt.
Module D – Coercive equalization and closure (steps 11–12): imposed suffering framed as restorative or equalizing and, at the extreme, destruction represented as necessary to collective restoration.
Collective threat or mobilization creates a demand for sacrifice.
Some persons visibly surrender life, bodily integrity, property, or ordinary life chances.
Sacrifice becomes a measure of virtue, loyalty, deservingness, or membership.
A second population is perceived as contributing less, profiting, avoiding risk, or surviving disproportionately.
The difference is narrated as sacrificial asymmetry rather than as an accidental or administratively necessary inequality.
Survival or lesser suffering is moralized: the alleged nonsacrificer becomes selfish, cowardly, parasitic, disloyal, or suspect.
The sacrificers’ losses are interpreted as creating a claim upon the nonsacrificer: sacrificial debt.
Political actors identify, generalize, and publicize the debtor category.
The accusation shifts from behavior to identity; counterevidence becomes less capable of discharging the debt.
The state or movement asserts authority to collect the debt through exclusion, extraction, forced labor, dispossession, imprisonment, or violence.
Under additional ideological and institutional conditions, imposed suffering is interpreted as restorative or equalizing.
At the extreme, the continued existence of the debtor population is represented as incompatible with collective redemption or survival.
5.2 The key transition: behavioral accusation to ontological accusation
The most promising theoretical threshold is the shift from behavior to identity. A behavioral accusation can in principle be remedied: enlist, serve, contribute, endure deprivation, or demonstrate loyalty. An ontological accusation cannot be remedied because the defect is assigned to the group’s essence. The German-Jewish veteran case is therefore crucial. Large numbers of Jews served in the German armed forces during the First World War, and many were wounded, decorated, or killed. Yet antisemitic accusations of Jewish shirking persisted and were institutionalized through the Judenzählung. Under Nazism, even demonstrable wartime sacrifice did not ultimately protect Jewish veterans as a category. This provides a concrete setting in which to test whether sacrificial debt becomes “unpayable” when the alleged deficit is racialized or essentialized.
5.3 Failed sacrifice as a possible accelerator
A second theoretical innovation concerns failed sacrifice. Successful sacrifice can be narrated as meaningful because the collective survives or wins. Catastrophic sacrifice followed by defeat produces a more difficult problem: either the sacrifice was unnecessary, the collective sacred object was false, leadership was incompetent, or some actor prevented the sacrifice from achieving its promised purpose. The last explanation preserves the moral value of the dead by relocating guilt. The project will therefore test whether failed collective sacrifice increases demand for traitors, shirkers, profiteers, internal enemies, or alleged beneficiaries of others’ suffering.

TABLE | Hypothesis | Claim | Observable implication
TABLE | H1 – Sacrificial essentialization | Resentment becomes more politically dangerous when nonsacrifice is attributed to group essence rather than remediable conduct. | Repeated claims that members cannot truly sacrifice or belong, even when counterexamples exist.
TABLE | H2 – Sacred-value intensity | The more sacred the collective object is represented to be, the more morally serious exemption from sacrifice becomes. | Rhetoric treating sacrifice as ultimate proof of belonging and survival as subordinate to collective preservation.

TABLE | H5 – Political entrepreneurship | Sacrificial resentment requires actors who name the debtor, connect survival to guilt, and propose sanctions. | Speeches, press, organizations, or policy entrepreneurs repeatedly constructing the debtor category.
TABLE | H6 – Institutional capacity | Escalation to coercive equalization requires institutions capable of identifying, isolating, dispossessing, transporting, imprisoning, or killing debtor populations. | Expansion of state capacity and policy instruments temporally preceding higher-order coercion.
TABLE | H7 – Debt non-dischargeability | Violence is more likely when actual contribution or sacrifice cannot erase the alleged debt. | Decorated veterans or demonstrably contributing members remain categorized as inherently suspect.

6.1 Rival explanations that must remain live
Racial antisemitism or ethnic hatred alone, without sacrificial accounting.
Scapegoating produced by defeat, economic crisis, or political instability.
Revenge for alleged betrayal in 1918.
Redemptive antisemitism in Friedländer’s sense, without a distinct debt mechanism.
Wartime brutalization and desensitization to killing.
Imperial conquest, Lebensraum, and anti-Bolshevik ideological warfare.
Bureaucratic radicalization, cumulative radicalization, and institutional competition.
Material expropriation and opportunistic incentives.
Security logic and counterinsurgency claims.
The project succeeds only if sacrificial debt explains something that these approaches do not, or if it specifies a mechanism through which several of them interact. It should not be presented as a total explanation of the Holocaust or of political violence generally.
7. Outcome Model: Coercive Escalation and Sacrificial-Debt Framing
The program should not collapse the severity of coercion into the political meaning assigned to it. Accordingly, the outcome model uses two related but analytically distinct dimensions: (1) coercive escalation and (2) sacrificial-debt framing. Genocide remains an extreme possible outcome, not the definition of sacrificial debt.
7.1 Coercive escalation
TABLE | Level | Coercive outcome | Operational description
TABLE | 0 | Recognition | Unequal burdens are recognized without moral condemnation or sanction.
TABLE | 1 | Informal stigma | Coward, shirker, profiteer, selfish, unpatriotic, disloyal; shaming or social pressure.
TABLE | 2 | Formal exclusion | Reduced standing, citizenship claims, access, employment, office, or membership.
TABLE | 3 | Extraction / dispossession | Special taxation, confiscation, forced contribution, labor demands, or deliberately unequal burdens.
TABLE | 4 | Detention / deportation / forced labor | Confinement, deportation, forced labor, deprivation, or systematic bodily coercion.
TABLE | 5 | Lethal persecution | Organized or tolerated killing directed at the alleged debtor population.
TABLE | 6 | Systematic extermination | Destruction of the group becomes a sustained policy or organized campaign.
7.2 Sacrificial-debt framing
TABLE | Level | Sacrificial-debt framing | Operational description
TABLE | 0 | Absent | No meaningful sacrificial comparison or debt logic.
TABLE | 1 | Sacrifice valorized | Sacrifice is praised or treated as evidence of duty, worth, or belonging.
TABLE | 2 | Asymmetry moralized | Unequal sacrifice is explicitly criticized and lesser suffering becomes morally suspect.
TABLE | 3 | Reciprocal obligation asserted | Because one constituency sacrificed, another is said to owe, pay, contribute, or lose protection.
TABLE | 4 | Debtor identity generalized | The obligation is attached to a category rather than only to particular conduct.
TABLE | 5 | Debt non-dischargeable | Actual service, loyalty, or sacrifice no longer reliably clears the accusation; imposed suffering may be represented as repayment, balance, justice, or equalization.
8. Research Design
8.1 Overall design
The project uses a nested comparative design: (1) mechanism discovery and process tracing in Germany; (2) structured, focused comparison with cases that exhibit mass sacrifice and resentment but different outcomes; and (3) later extension to additional cases only after the mechanism and codebook prove stable. This sequencing avoids the common error of building a universal theory from one extreme case or expanding the corpus before the variables are interpretable.
8.2 Initial case selection
The initial cases are not symmetrical controls. They perform different methodological functions within a structured, focused comparison. Germany is a revealing mechanism-discovery case; Britain, Australia, and France are contrast cases selected to probe different components of the proposed mechanism and to identify where escalation stops or changes form.
TABLE | Case | Function | Relevant features | Analytical use
TABLE | Germany | Mechanism discovery / revealing case | Mass casualties; defeat; cult of the fallen; Judenzählung; racial antisemitism; Nazi seizure of state; Holocaust. | Discover the mechanism and trace how sacrificial comparison may become essentialized, racialized, non-dischargeable, and coercive.
TABLE | Britain | Contrast: individualized stigma and social coercion | Mass mobilization; white-feather campaigns; “shirkers”; conscientious objectors; strong stigma and coercion. | Test how far sacrificial policing can intensify when accusations remain primarily individualized rather than becoming a fixed group debt.
TABLE | Australia | Contrast: explicit equality-of-sacrifice rhetoric | Voluntary enlistment crisis; conscription referenda; public naming/shaming; explicit equality-of-sacrifice arguments. | Provide the cleanest test of explicit reciprocal burden-sharing language without an equivalent trajectory toward essentialized debtor identity.
TABLE | France | Contrast: republican burden-sharing / blood tax | Very high casualties; republican “blood tax”; embusqués; industrial exemptions; profiteer resentment. | Test how a civic order manages strong burden-sharing claims and resentment toward exemption without constructing the same form of racialized, non-dischargeable debt.
8.3 Deferred comparative cases
Possible second-wave cases include the Ottoman Empire, Italy, Imperial Japan, revolutionary or civil-war Russia, postwar paramilitary movements, and selected ethnic-cleansing cases. The Ottoman case is especially promising because scholarship already uses “social relations of sacrifice,” but its inclusion would immediately introduce the Armenian genocide and therefore should occur only after the framework is robust enough to avoid circular case selection. The project should resist adding cases simply because they are dramatic.

10. Evidence Model and Codebook
The unit of analysis should initially be the proposition, not the document. A single speech may contain several distinct claims and should be coded at the smallest meaningful level capable of preserving who sacrifices, who benefits, who owes, and what sanction is proposed.
10.1 Minimum evidence rule and emic/etic distinction
Sacrificial debt should be coded as more than sacrificial asymmetry only when three elements are jointly present: (A) an identifiable prior or ongoing sacrifice; (B) an explicit comparison with a less-sacrificing, spared, or benefiting actor; and (C) a normative implication that the second actor therefore owes something, deserves less, must contribute, or may legitimately be sanctioned. Without element C, the evidence supports sacrificial asymmetry but not necessarily debt.
Emic sacrificial debt refers to historical actors’ own language of owing, payment, repayment, balance, equal burden, “must also,” “cannot remain spared,” or close equivalents. Etic sacrificial debt refers to a researcher’s reconstruction of a creditor/debtor relationship when such language is not explicit. Emic and etic evidence must be coded separately and must not be treated as evidentially equivalent.

TABLE | audience | Intended audience if known
TABLE | collective_object | Nation, Volk, state, revolution, race, etc.
TABLE | sacrificer | Who is represented as suffering or giving?
TABLE | sacrifice_type | Death, injury, military service, food, property, labor, family, freedom
TABLE | alleged_survivor | Who is represented as spared, safe, profiting, or avoiding?
TABLE | explicit_comparison | Are sacrificer and survivor directly juxtaposed? yes/no/uncertain

10.2 Evidence-strength rules
TABLE | Strength | Minimum interpretation
TABLE | Weak | Generic praise of sacrifice or generic hostility toward an out-group with no comparison or debt logic.
TABLE | Moderate | Sacrificers and alleged nonsacrificers are compared; unequal burden is morally criticized.
TABLE | Strong | Explicit claim that because one group suffers or dies, another group must contribute, suffer, pay, or lose protection.
TABLE | Decisive for mechanism | Repeated, temporally situated evidence linking prior/ongoing sacrifice to coercive action against the alleged debtor, combined with evidence that countervailing sacrifice does not discharge the debt.
10.3 Inference guardrails
Do not code ordinary antisemitic or racist statements as sacrificial debt unless the statement also invokes unequal contribution, suffering, survival, deservingness, repayment, or equivalent moral accounting.
Do not infer causation from the mere coexistence of high casualties and persecution.
Distinguish rhetorical legitimation from operational causation. A regime may justify violence through sacrifice language even if other factors drive policy formation.
Distinguish voluntary offering, legal conscription, social coercion, forced labor, and exterminatory victimization rather than collapsing all bodily surrender into one category.
Preserve chronology. A later statement cannot establish that an earlier decision was motivated by the same logic unless independent evidence supports continuity.
Record counterexamples, exceptions, and actor disagreement. Nazi ideology was not perfectly coherent, and the project should not manufacture coherence through selective quotation.
Avoid treating Jewish victims as passive symbols. Include Jewish veterans’ self-understandings, appeals to service, and responses to exclusion wherever sources permit.
11. Falsification Strategy and Negative Evidence
A dedicated falsification file should be part of the repository from day one. The purpose is not merely to list limitations at publication time but to make the project actively search for evidence that would weaken its preferred interpretation.
TABLE | Test | Potential disconfirming evidence | Implication
TABLE | F1 | Hitler’s “balance” or reciprocal-death language proves isolated rather than recurrent and has little relationship to anti-Jewish policy or broader Nazi discourse. | Downgrade Koenigsberg-derived mechanism from central to personal/rhetorical motif.
TABLE | F2 | German Jewish military sacrifice consistently protected veterans and successfully discharged accusations of nonsacrifice. | Weakens unpayable-debt and essentialization hypotheses.
TABLE | F3 | Comparable societies display equally strong ontological nonsacrifice accusations without greater coercive escalation. | Weakens essentialization as discriminating causal condition.
TABLE | F4 | Escalation occurs before or independently of casualty shocks, defeat, or sacrificial comparison. | Weakens magnitude and failed-sacrifice hypotheses.
TABLE | F5 | Primary actors explain coercion entirely through security, race, conquest, or material logic and do not connect it to differential sacrifice. | Sacrificial debt may be secondary legitimation rather than causal mechanism.
TABLE | F6 | Groups regarded as low-sacrifice are reintegrated through demonstrable service in cases where the theory predicts non-dischargeability. | Requires narrower scope conditions for ontological debt.
TABLE | F7 | The same rhetoric appears routinely in nonviolent settings but has no predictive relationship to exclusion or coercion. | Sacrificial debt may describe moral language without explaining escalation.
12. Methods
12.1 Qualitative process tracing
The German case should be analyzed through process tracing designed to identify the temporal sequence from mass sacrifice to group-specific accusation to policy escalation. Evidence should be organized around hypothesized causal links rather than around personalities alone. Especially valuable are “smoking-gun” passages that explicitly connect a prior or ongoing sacrifice to the legitimacy of imposing suffering on another group.
12.2 Structured, focused comparison
Each case should answer the same questions:
What collective object demanded sacrifice?
Who visibly bore the greatest losses?
Which persons or groups were accused of evading sacrifice?
Was the accusation behavioral or essentialized?
Could actual contribution discharge the accusation?
Who narrated the inequality as morally unjust?
What sanctions were proposed or imposed?
What institutions constrained or enabled escalation?
Did defeat or failed sacrifice intensify the rhetoric?
What was the maximum observed level on the coercive-escalation scale, and what was the highest observed level of sacrificial-debt framing?
12.3 Discourse analysis
Discourse analysis will identify recurring metaphors and moral oppositions: blood versus profit, front versus rear, giver versus taker, hero versus parasite, dead versus survivor, creditor versus debtor, purity versus contamination, and sacrifice versus self-preservation. The objective is not to treat language as transparent evidence of mental state, but to reconstruct the public moral grammar through which coercion becomes intelligible and defensible.
12.4 Computational methods: later, not first
Automated corpus methods should be deferred until manual coding produces stable concepts. Once the codebook is reliable, computational work can assist with retrieval, temporal frequency analysis, co-occurrence networks, actor comparison, and discovery of candidate passages. A model might search for constructions combining a sacrificer term (“soldiers,” “front,” “blood,” “fallen”) with a survivor/debtor term (“shirkers,” “profiteers,” “Jews,” “safe,” “spared,” “pay”) and a normative operator (“must,” “owe,” “also,” “cannot be allowed,” “justice”). Machine retrieval must not substitute for source criticism or interpretive coding.
12.5 Causal-role discipline
The program must distinguish evidence that sacrificial debt helps constitute political membership from evidence that it diagnoses an alleged debtor, motivates punishment, legitimates an existing policy, contributes to escalation, or directly affects a policy decision. These roles are related but not interchangeable; a finding that sacrificial debt functioned primarily as a classificatory or legitimating mechanism would remain theoretically significant without establishing it as an independent cause of mass violence.
Constitutive: sacrificial reasoning helps define who counts as a worthy member of the political community.
Diagnostic: sacrificial reasoning identifies who allegedly failed or benefited at the collective’s expense.
Motivational: sacrificial reasoning supplies a reason actors desire punishment or exclusion.
Legitimating: sacrificial reasoning makes an existing or proposed sanction appear just, reciprocal, or deserved.
Escalatory: sacrificial reasoning helps make more severe sanctions acceptable or necessary.
Decision-causal: evidence shows that sacrificial reasoning materially influenced a concrete policy decision.

13.1 Pilot go/no-go criteria
At least two independent coders can distinguish sacrificial-debt evidence from generic hostility with acceptable agreement.
German sources contain more than isolated examples of explicit reciprocal moral accounting.
Jewish military sacrifice provides observable tests of whether debt could be discharged.
Comparative cases contain strong sacrificial-equality rhetoric but diverge meaningfully in essentialization and coercive outcomes.
At least one hypothesis is weakened, revised, or rejected by the pilot; if every hypothesis is “confirmed,” the coding rules are likely too permissive.
The literature review still identifies a real conceptual gap after searching adjacent terms such as moral economy, deservingness, debt, reciprocity, blood tax, sacrificial citizenship, social relations of sacrifice, and redemptive violence.
14. Formal Novelty Audit
Because adjacent literatures are extensive, originality must be demonstrated rather than asserted. The project should create a short “novelty matrix” covering the following bodies of scholarship: First World War social history; nationalism and memorialization; moral economies of sacrifice and citizenship; veterans and welfare; Holocaust intentionalist/functional/radicalization debates; redemptive antisemitism; genocide theory; scapegoating; collective guilt; resentment and distributive justice; political theology; and anthropological work on debt and sacrifice.
For each literature, record:
Does it explicitly theorize unequal sacrifice?
Does it describe a creditor/debtor relationship?
Does it make survival itself morally suspect?
Does it theorize the transition from behavior to ontological group guilt?
Does it explain why demonstrated sacrifice may fail to discharge the debt?
Does it connect perceived unpaid sacrifice to coercive suffering or killing?
Does it offer comparative scope conditions explaining escalation versus containment?
The project’s strongest potential novelty lies not in any one component, but in specifying the full transition from asymmetry to debt to essentialization to coercive collection, and then testing the transition across cases.
15. Ethical and Historiographical Safeguards
Research on the Holocaust requires special methodological restraint. The concept of “sacrifice” must never imply that Jewish victims consented to, participated in, or shared the political meaning imposed by perpetrators. “Coercive sacrificial equalization” is a hypothesis about perpetrator or political meaning, not a description of the victims’ own status or experience.
Do not equate military combat deaths with genocide. The project compares political logics of bodily obligation while maintaining categorical differences in agency, targeting, legality, and victimization.
Do not treat perpetrator explanations as moral justification. The research asks how killing became meaningful to perpetrators and publics, not whether the meaning was valid.
Do not reduce the Holocaust to a single causal mechanism. Racial antisemitism, conquest, state institutions, cumulative radicalization, collaboration, and contingency remain indispensable.
Use victim and survivor sources to prevent the analysis from reproducing the perpetrator’s symbolic world as if it were the entire historical reality.
Label speculative inferential steps explicitly and reserve strongest claims for repeated primary-source evidence.
Distinguish empirical claims about what actors said from theoretical interpretations of why those statements mattered.

20. Principal Research Risks
TABLE | Risk | Failure mode | Mitigation
TABLE | Confirmation bias toward Koenigsberg | The corpus is selected to reproduce “balance the scales of death.” | Pre-register rival explanations; maintain negative-evidence file; include independent coders.
TABLE | Concept inflation | Every wartime complaint becomes “sacrificial debt.” | Require explicit asymmetry + moral obligation indicators for strong coding.
TABLE | Teleology | WWI evidence is read as inevitably leading to the Holocaust. | Use negative cases and period-appropriate uncertainty; preserve alternative pathways.
TABLE | False equivalence | Combat death and genocide appear morally or historically equivalent. | State categorical differences in agency and targeting in every major output.
TABLE | Single-actor psychology | Hitler’s rhetoric is mistaken for a society-wide mechanism. | Compare Goebbels, Himmler, institutions, propaganda, veterans, and publics.
TABLE | Overclaiming novelty | Existing scholarship already contains the same theory. | Complete novelty audit before publication claims.
TABLE | Automation too early | NLP scales a weak codebook and produces false precision. | Manual pilot and coder agreement before computational expansion.
TABLE | Outcome determinism | Sacrificial resentment is treated as a direct cause of genocide. | Use escalation scale and scope conditions; model institutional and ideological moderators.
21. Expected Contributions
21.1 To political pathology
The project would identify a mechanism by which political communities transform uneven loss into moral hierarchy. It would provide diagnostic questions for recognizing when sacrifice ceases to be merely a demand placed on members and becomes a claim against those represented as insufficiently sacrificial.
21.2 To war and nationalism studies
The project would connect literatures on the fallen soldier, wartime deservingness, citizenship, and social conflict to the subsequent treatment of alleged nonsacrificers. It asks not only how the dead are sanctified but what happens politically to the living who are compared with them.
21.3 To Holocaust historiography
The project would not propose sacrificial debt as a replacement for established explanations. Its contribution would be narrower: to test whether reciprocal moral accounting helps explain why Jewish survival, alleged exemption, and German military sacrifice could be placed in the same justificatory frame by Hitler and other Nazi actors, and whether the persistence of antisemitic suspicion despite Jewish military service reveals a transition to non-dischargeable group guilt.
21.4 To comparative political violence
The project would offer a framework for asking why similar languages of unequal sacrifice sometimes stop at stigma and sometimes contribute to state coercion. This could eventually be tested in revolutions, civil wars, ethnic conflicts, veteran politics, and other settings where one constituency claims to have “paid” while another allegedly survived, profited, or abstained.
22. Provisional Theoretical Thesis
Political communities become especially dangerous when sacrifice is treated as proof of superior moral worth. Once the dead are creditors, the living can become debtors. If a population is represented as having escaped the collective blood price, survival itself may be recoded as evidence of guilt. When that debt is essentialized and made impossible to discharge through ordinary service or loyalty, political authority may claim the right to collect it through coercive suffering.
This is a proposition to be tested, narrowed, and potentially rejected. Its value lies precisely in making the chain of reasoning explicit enough that evidence can break it.
23. What Would Count as a Successful Project?
A concept of sacrificial debt that is clearly distinguishable from existing notions of scapegoating, prejudice, reciprocity, deservingness, and revenge.
A transparent codebook that another researcher can apply to the same primary sources.
Evidence that identifies both where the mechanism appears and where it fails.
A comparative explanation for variation in escalation, not merely a compelling reading of Hitler.
A revised account of Koenigsberg’s contribution that identifies precisely what he discovered and what the new project adds.
A body of negative evidence strong enough that the final theory contains explicit scope conditions.
Scholarly outputs that can be defended without relying on the reader accepting the Foundation’s broader theory of the nation as sacred object.

25. Preliminary Evidence Notes for the Proposal
Several sources are especially important because they establish that the project begins from documented historical patterns rather than from analogy alone:
The International Encyclopedia of the First World War describes a wartime “community of sacrifice” that excluded shirkers and profiteers, and states that acceptance of hardship depended on its equal distribution.
Its article on the German Judenzählung explains that the 1916 census was driven by unfounded accusations that Jews were evading frontline service and notes that stereotypes of Jewish unwillingness to self-sacrifice for the Fatherland were turned into a political weapon.
The Australia conscription entry explicitly records the argument that the burden of war should be equitably shared and formulates the grievance as “why should my son, father or husband die while yours lives?”
The British citizenship literature records intense public hostility toward men labeled shirkers and toward conscientious objectors, including public shaming.
French labor history describes military service as an egalitarian “blood tax” and documents resentment toward embusqués who appeared to escape the front.
Koenigsberg’s “Sacrificial Meaning of the Holocaust” explicitly argues that German soldiers were primary sacrificial victims for Hitler and quotes Hitler reasoning that a leader willing to send valuable German blood into war possessed the right to destroy allegedly inferior populations.
Michael Geheran’s work on Jewish WWI veterans provides a direct empirical bridge between wartime sacrifice, claims to Germanness, and the later racial state.
26. Final Recommendation
Proceed with the project as a comparative theory-building research program rather than as a Holocaust-only validation exercise or a proposal for a single paper. The immediate objective should not be to prove that Koenigsberg was right. It should be to determine whether his observation reveals a general mechanism—sacrificial debt—that can be defined independently, detected in primary sources, distinguished from sacrificial asymmetry and rival explanations, falsified, and used to explain variation across political systems. Within that program, designate “When Survival Becomes Guilt: World War I, Jewish Military Service, and the Making of an Unpayable Debt” as the recommended postgraduate empirical paper because it subjects the non-dischargeability claim to a focused historical test.
Koenigsberg supplies the generative observation. The Foundation’s contribution should be to identify the mechanism, define its scope, explain its escalation, and show where it does not operate.

Appendix B. Core Diagnostic Questions for Any Case
1. What must be preserved, redeemed, purified, or made real through sacrifice?
2. Who is asked or compelled to give?
3. What exactly is surrendered?
4. Who interprets the surrender as sacrifice?
5. Who is represented as having escaped the burden?
6. Is the inequality factual, perceived, fabricated, or deliberately exaggerated?
7. Is survival itself contrasted with the suffering of others?
8. Does the sacrificer become morally superior because of the sacrifice?
9. Does the survivor become morally inferior because of survival?
10. Is an obligation of repayment stated or implied, and is the debt language emic (actor-stated) or etic (researcher-reconstructed)?
11. Can the alleged debtor discharge the obligation through service or contribution?
12. Does the debt attach to individuals or to an inherited/essentialized group identity?
13. Who claims standing to collect the debt?
14. What institutional mechanisms are available to impose payment?
15. At what point does punishment become represented as justice, balance, redemption, purification, or equalization, and what role does that framing play: classificatory, motivational, legitimating, escalatory, or decision-causal?
16. What evidence shows that the mechanism did not operate, stopped, or was resisted?
